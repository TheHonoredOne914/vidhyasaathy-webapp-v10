One final orientation note on tone. VidhyaSaathy is built around a simple promise made to a tired Class-9 student: "You don't
need to decide what to study tonight. The app already knows, it knows how you learn, and it will walk with you." Every section
that follows is just that promise, made engineering-real — the diagnostic that finds what you actually know, the bands that
route you to what actually helps, the cloud companion that removes the chaos, and the evidence trail that proves to your
teacher and your parents that you are moving. Keep that promise in mind whenever a decision feels ambiguous; it settles
most arguments.

PA R T 1

Product Charter
What VidhyaSaathy is, the problem it kills, why the app itself — not any single feature — is the moat, and exactly what
"ready" means at the end of the four-day build.

  1.1 — What VidhyaSaathy Is
VidhyaSaathy is a school-anchored learning companion for Indian students of Class 9 and 10, built on NCERT content, in
which the school's own structure — classes, sections, roll numbers, subjects, teachers — is the backbone of the product.
The app mirrors the school instead of replacing it: teachers publish what they taught, students get one clear, adaptive path
through it, parents finally see real progress in plain language, and the admin runs the whole world from one console. Four
roles, one shared truth: every piece of content, every quiz attempt, every doubt and every plan lives against the same
concept map, so evidence flows from the student's screen to the teacher's heatmap to the parent's weekly summary without
anyone re-entering anything.
The product's engine is deliberately simple and deliberately honest: diagnose first, teach second, adapt always. Before a
student is taught anything, a short diagnostic identifies what they already know; the four performance bands (0-25%, 25-
50%, 50-75%, 75-100%) then route them to the explanation depth, practice difficulty and pacing that fits; and as they work,
every answer — including how confident they felt answering it — feeds back into per-concept mastery scores that re-route
the next session. Nothing in the app is decorative: every screen either teaches, tests, adapts, reports, or removes chaos.

  1.2 — The Problem We Kill
The enemy is not ignorance; it is chaos. A Class-9 student preparing for a Physics test today is lost between a YouTube video
of unknown quality, a PDF of someone else's notes, three WhatsApp groups, a friend's photocopy, and a coaching institute's
timetable — with no idea which one matches what their teacher taught, their syllabus, and their actual gaps. Parents cannot
help because they cannot see inside the chaos; they only see marks. Teachers cannot help after 5 pm because they have no
view of what each child actually struggled with at home.


    "The problem was never that the student is weak. The problem is that 17 different sources are shouting
    at once and none of them knows the student."

VidhyaSaathy's answer is structural, not cosmetic: one Next-Action card that tells the student the single right thing to do
tonight, one curated set of resources that the teacher approved for this exact concept, one cloud companion that answers
doubts at 11 pm without judgement, and one progress trail that turns effort into visible proof for parents and teachers.
Removing the chaos — not adding more content — is the product's core value, and it is why the home screen will always be
quieter than any competitor's.

  1.3 — The Moat Statement
Features are copyable; the loop is not. Any competitor can ship quizzes, flashcards and an AI chatbot in a quarter. What they
cannot copy is the compounding data flywheel that this app is designed around from day one, inside one real school, on one
real board (CBSE/NCERT), with teachers publishing daily and students practising nightly:

  1.5 — What "Ready in 4 Days" Means
"Ready" is defined before building begins, and it is deliberately a product definition, not a technology one. At the end of Day
4 the following sentence must be true end-to-end, demonstrated live on a real phone: a school admin has set up the school
and issued activation codes; a teacher has published tonight's session for Class 9-A with a diagnostic, notes and a quiz;
three different students — strong, struggling and absent — have signed in, been routed differently by their bands, completed
the session, asked the cloud companion a doubt, and played one game; and a parent has opened a dashboard with real
graphs generated from those exact events. Every checklist item in Part 10 exists to make that one sentence true, and no work
that does not serve it belongs in the four days.

   Non-negotiable quality bar: "ready" means working flows with real data — server-side scoring, real database reads and

  2.2 — The School-Anchored Identity Model
Nobody "creates an account" in the casual consumer sense. The app is a mirror of the school's real structure, so identity
flows from it: the admin creates the school, academic year, classes and sections; teachers are invited by staff ID and email;
students activate with a code plus their roll number and date of birth; parents are linked to their child via an OTP after school
approval. This anchoring is what makes every later feature trustworthy — a "Class 9-A Physics session" means a real
teacher's real session for a real section, not a generic course. It is also the security backbone: class membership, subject
assignments and parent-child links are database facts that row-level security checks on every request, so a parent can never
read another child's data and a student can never read answer keys.

  3.2 — Diagnostic-First Rule (Test Before Teach)
Before a student is taught any new chapter, they take a short diagnostic — roughly five questions drawn from the chapter's
prerequisite chain plus a taste of the new material. The result places each concept on the student's mastery map and
decides the entry band: the 0-25% student gets prerequisite repair first, while the 75-100% student is challenged
immediately. The diagnostic is framed to the student as a friendly "let's see what you already know — this is not a test," it is
never graded, and it is never shown to parents as marks. Its output is routing, not judgement.
This rule exists because teaching a student what they already know wastes their scarce 40 minutes and quietly insults them,
while teaching over a broken prerequisite guarantees that tonight's effort evaporates by morning. The diagnostic is cheap
(five questions, three minutes) and its payoff is the entire personalisation premise. Every new chapter, for every student,
starts with a diagnostic — no exceptions, including chapters the teacher is confident about.

  3.3 — The Next-Action Card (Chaos Removal, Made Literal)
The student home screen is dominated by a single card that answers the only question that matters at 8 pm: "what should I
do right now?" The card is computed server-side from the evidence trail — an unfinished session outranks everything; a due-
tomorrow session outranks revision; revision of a decaying concept outranks new content; new content outranks games. It
shows the concept, the estimated time, and one tap takes the student into exactly that activity mid-state. The rest of the
home screen stays deliberately sparse: streak chip, one encouragement line, and quiet access to the rest of the world.
Competing products fill this space with feeds; VidhyaSaathy fills it with calm, because the product promise in Part 1 was "you
don't need to decide."

  3.5 — The Evidence Trail
Every learning event is appended to one immutable log — quiz answers with confidence buttons and time-to-answer, game

corrections are new events. This trail is what makes every downstream screen honest at zero extra teacher effort: the
heatmap is an aggregation of the trail, the parent graphs are an aggregation of the trail, the band routing is a query over the
trail, and the AI's context window is the recent slice of the trail. It is also the moat's raw material (Part 1.3) — which is why the
trail is designed and built on Day 1, before any screen that displays it.

The Four Student Bands
The heart of personalisation: four internal performance bands — 0-25%, 25-50%, 50-75%, 75-100% — that silently
route every child to the explanation depth, difficulty, pacing and tone that fits them, and move them up the moment they
earn it.

  4.1 — Two Non-Negotiable Rules (Read First)
   Bands are internal, forever. A student never sees a band label, a percentage rank, or any wording that smells like one. The student-
   visible vocabulary is only: "Mastered", "Almost there", "Let's fix this", "Top form". No leaderboards exist anywhere in the product —
   this is a permanent strategic cut, not a missing feature.
   People are never labelled — concepts are. Bands are a routing mechanism over per-concept mastery, not a verdict on a child. The
   same student is routinely Band-4 on "Distance" and Band-1 on "Acceleration", and the app must be equally comfortable serving both
   in one session.

  4.3 — How Band Placement Is Computed
Band placement is deliberately simple, explainable and per-concept: the diagnostic seeds an initial mastery score per
concept; every subsequent quiz answer updates it (correct-at-high-difficulty counts more; "Guess" confidence discounts a
correct answer heavily; "Sure" + wrong is treated as a real misconception, the most diagnostic signal in the app); a rolling
window (last ~10 interactions per concept) smooths noise. The band for a chapter is then derived from its prerequisite-
weighted average. There is no black-box model in the prototype — simple, honest arithmetic the team can inspect, explain to
a teacher, and debug at 11 pm. Sophistication can come later; trust must exist from day one.

  4.4 — Promotion, Demotion and the Difficulty Ladder
Every question carries a difficulty tag — L1 basic, L2 medium, L3 application — and the ladder rule is fixed product law: a
student starts at L2; three consecutive correct climb to L3; two consecutive wrong drop to L1, always with a bridge
explanation so the drop is felt as help, not punishment. Promotion between bands follows sustained evidence (e.g. L2-L3
correctness at ≥70% across the chapter's concepts plus "Sure" confidence on most), and every transition is written to the
log so a teacher can see "Aarav moved 25-50 → 50-75 on Motion, Tuesday" — progress made visible is the product's
favourite kind of motivation. Demotion after absences or decayed mastery is quiet and shame-free: the app simply serves
bridge content and never announces a fall.

  5.3 — How the AI Predicts the Way Each Child Learns
The prediction layer is intentionally legible: it observes which explanation formats correlate with this child's subsequent
correctness and shifts serving weights over time. If Priya answers consistently better after worked examples with everyday
analogies, examples rise in her variant mix and dense theory sinks; if Aarav retains better after testing himself (flashcards and
Dushman questions) than after re-reading, his Next-Action leans practice-first. The weights start neutral, move slowly, and
are always inspectable in the profile ("explains best with: examples and Hindi terms; consolidates by: self-testing"). The AI
never claims a scientific learning-style theory; it just stops serving what demonstrably does not work for this child. Prediction
here means pattern memory with a paper trail, and that is a feature — teachers and parents can ask "why did it show her
that?" and get a real answer.

  5.4 — The Cloud Companion (Mascot, Chat and Plan-Maker)
The cloud mascot is the AI's face and the student's constant, calm companion — the one place help always lives. It is a
friendly blue cloud with a small book, present in the chat and in micro-reactions across the student world, with eight
restrained behaviour states: idle (subtle float), greeting, thinking (while the AI processes), encouragement after meaningful
progress, confusion-support when the student is stuck, celebration at milestones, resting while the student reads or solves,
and error (something failed — never the student's fault). Hard rules inherited from the brand: the cloud never animates
distractingly during question-solving, never flashes for attention, never uses emotional pressure to keep a child online, and
honours reduced-motion device settings everywhere.
In chat, the companion answers doubts in the student's chosen language (English, Hindi, or natural Hinglish), asks one
clarifying question when the doubt is vague, and answers at the student's band depth — the same doubt about acceleration
gets an analogy-first answer for Priya and a formula-forward answer for Aarav. As plan-maker it does the chaos removal of Part
1 literally: "exam in 3 weeks, 6 concepts shaky, 25 minutes a night — here is the plan", generated from the evidence trail and
always realistic (plans that assume a suddenly-perfect student are lies, and children know it). Every conversation is private to
the student by the privacy wall of Part 2.4.

                                                                  10
  5.5 — AI Safety Rules (Non-Negotiable)
   No fabricated citations. The AI never invents book page numbers, links or references. Content anchors only to concepts and
   resources that exist in the school's own library.
   The honest "I don't know." When unsure, the companion says so plainly and offers the doubt-escalation path — a wrong-but-
   confident answer from a tutor destroys the product's core trust.
   Homework mode is Socratic. If the teacher flagged the session as homework, the AI never hands the direct answer; it guides with
   questions and hints, then encourages the student's own attempt.
   AI-drafted content is visually separate and teacher-gated. AI-drafted practice questions appear in a distinct "AI draft" style and
   never reach students without explicit teacher approval.
   Keys stay server-side. The student client never receives correct-answer flags for quizzes — scoring happens on the server,
   always.
   Tone guardrails. No fear, no shame, no comparison with other children — encoded in the companion's system prompt and spot-
   checked in QA.

  5.7 — The 11-Step Server-Side Request Sequence
Every AI chat turn follows one fixed server-side sequence (Edge Function, keys never in the client): 1) auth check → 2) rate-
limit + token budget check → 3) load student context slice from the evidence trail → 4) load concept map around the doubt
→ 5) detect language and band depth → 6) assemble system prompt with safety rules and tone profile → 7) stream the
answer (SSE) with the companion's thinking state → 8) log the interaction privately → 9) update doubt-cluster counters
(anonymised) → 10) write any promised follow-ups (Dushman queue, plan items) → 11) close budget accounting. The
sequence is deliberately spelled out here because it is the difference between an AI feature and an AI liability: no step may
be skipped, and none of it runs on the student's phone.

The app is only as good as the NCERT fuel inside it. This part defines the concept map, the question bank spec, the
content quality bar, and the seeded demo world used for every test and demo.

  8.1 — The Concept Map (the product's skeleton)
Content lives in a strict hierarchy — School → Year → Class → Subject → Chapter → Concept — and every concept carries
prerequisite edges. The prerequisite graph is not decoration: the
                                                              24 diagnostic draws from it, the repair paths walk backwards
along it, the study plans sequence by it, and the "concepts are labelled, people are not" rule reads from it. For the prototype,
the map is hand-built with teacher review for the seeded chapters, starting with the Motion chain.

                                                                   lives here — perfect Error DNA demo

  Acceleration                 Speed & average speed               Sign convention confusion, graph reading, the highest-doubt

  8.2 — Question Bank Specification
Every question in the bank carries four mandatory tags — concept, difficulty (L1/L2/L3), board format, and provenance —
plus its options, its correct answer (server-side only), and its error-family hints (which wrong option indicates which
confusion). The prototype needs the seeded chapters fully banked, roughly:

  Per seeded concept                      Diagnostic-grade         Practice pool             Format mix target

                                                                                             Reason 15% · Case-based 10% · Short 5%

                                                                                             misconception pattern

  Acceleration                            1 probe + 2              ~30 questions             Same mix, heavier on sign convention
                                          prerequisite pulls                                 and graph reading


Quality bar for every question: unambiguous stem, exactly one defensibly correct answer, distractors that mean something
(each maps to a real confusion), bilingual-capable (stem at minimum in English and Hindi), and no trick questions — difficulty
must come from thinking, not wording. Ten excellent questions beat thirty mediocre ones, because every bad question
corrupts the evidence trail the whole moat stands on.

   Teacher-authored notes for the three seeded concepts (Sharma Ma'am's actual emphasis, ~1 page each, with 2-3 checkpoint

   Summary one-pager PDF for the Motion chapter — definitions, core formulas, two classic exam patterns, mini concept map.

9.3 — Daily Rhythm (the discipline that makes 4 days possible)
 One phase at a time, fully. The AI builder receives a phase, answers its question gate, builds, verifies its own checklist, reports —
 then and only then the next phase. Stacking phases is how static-HTML chaos was born; do not revive it.
 Test as the user, not as the developer. Every exit demo is walked on a real (preferably low-end) phone with the seeded accounts,
 in both languages at least once a day.
 No regressions. Each day ends with a five-minute regression pass of the previous days' exit demos. A feature that breaks
 yesterday's demo is not a new feature; it is a bug.
 Honest triage over silent scope creep. Anything slipping past its day goes through the 9.4 table explicitly — in the open, recorded,
 never by quietly shipping it half-broken.

Quality Gates & Final Testing
The global definition of done, the full-day acceptance script that proves the product, the edge-case sweep, and the two
fix rounds that turn "mostly working" into demo-ready.

  11.1 — Global Definition of Done (applies to every phase, every day)
   Real data, real flows. Every list, score, graph and notification is computed from the database on a real role's session — no
   placeholder JSON, no mock logins, no "will wire later".
   Both languages. Anything a user can read exists in English and Hindi with zero missing keys at the moment it ships.
   All states designed. Loading, empty, error + retry, and permission-denied states exist for every async surface — the skeleton is
   part of the feature.
   Security proven, not assumed. Each new capability gets one positive test (right role succeeds) and one negative test (wrong role
   rejected, in the database, not just hidden in UI).
   Evidence written. If the feature touches learning, it writes to the trail with the right event shape — or it is a screen, not a feature.
   No regressions. Previous exit demos still pass before the phase is called done.

  11.4 — The Two Fix Rounds
After the acceptance script, the calendar holds exactly two fix rounds — Round 1 for everything that broke the script or the
checklist (severity first: data integrity, security, then flows, then polish), and Round 2 for the fallout of Round 1 plus the triage
decisions from 9.4 made real (deferred items moved to the backlog properly, half-built features either finished or hidden,
never left dangling). After Round 2 the build freezes: the remaining hours go to rehearsal and rest, because a tired demo of a
working app loses to a fresh demo of a slightly-less-working one, every single time.

The Moat — Why This Compounds
What is deliberately never built, what the flywheel becomes after launch, and the order of the backlog that follows.

  12.1 — What We Deliberately Never Build
Strategy is what you say no to, so the cuts are recorded as product law, not as TODOs in disguise: no public leaderboards or
any classmate-vs-classmate comparison (motivation here is personal — streaks, badges, beaten enemies); no coaching-
institute integrations or ERP/fees/transport modules (the school's learning world is the boundary); no camera or microphone
emotion detection (cut forever — creepy, unreliable, and it would poison the trust the privacy wall exists to build); no AR/VR
in this horizon. Every one of these was weighed and refused with reasons; when someone requests them in month three, this
section is the answer.

  12.2 — What the Flywheel Becomes
After the four days, the app is not finished — it is armed. Every night it learns the school a little better: which concepts
produce which confusions in this section, which explanations rescue which kinds of errors, which students are quietly afraid
of which chapters. Six months of that is a dataset no competitor can buy and a rapport no generic app can fake — teachers'
reteaches get pre-suggested, parents' conversations get grounded, students' plans get personal. The features were never
the moat; the compounding knowledge of each child, inside one trusted school is. That is why Part 3's evidence trail was

built before any beautiful screen, and why every new idea must answer "which station of the loop does this serve?" before it
earns an hour.

  12.3 — Post-Launch Backlog (in priority order, after real usage)
 1. Signature AI suite completion — the deeper doubt tooling and assistive surfaces that were consciously deferred so the core loop
    shipped honest and fast.
 2. Recordings pipeline — class-recording segments with school permission controls, closing the last content gap for absent students.
 3. PIN profiles for shared phones — because many households share one device, and the streak must survive it.
 4. WhatsApp fallback notifications — meeting parents where they already live, with the same two-notices-a-week discipline.
 5. Advanced teacher tooling — deeper analytics exports, co-teaching views, department rollups.
 6. Second school — only when the first one runs itself for a month. Multi-tenancy before repeatability is how pilots die.
